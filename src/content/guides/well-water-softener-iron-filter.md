Municipal city pages on SoftenerIndex are about **utility water**. A private well is different: chemistry varies by aquifer, casing, nearby land use, and even season. SoftenerIndex will not invent a grains-per-gallon figure, an iron ppm cutoff that “always” needs a filter, or a package price for a softener-plus-iron stack. This page is quote literacy for well owners who keep hearing “you need both.”

## Start with a lab result, not a brochure

The [U.S. EPA’s private-well guidance](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) says well owners should test annually for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH, and should test for other substances when conditions suggest them. Two rows on EPA’s testing chart matter for this guide:

- **Stained plumbing fixtures or laundry** → consider testing for iron, copper, and manganese.
- **Scaly residues / soaps that don’t lather** → hardness.
- **A softener is being proposed for hardness** → EPA also flags manganese and iron as substances to consider.

EPA also points well owners to **state-certified laboratories** (and local health departments) rather than a salesperson’s free “in-home test” as the only data. SoftenerIndex keeps a placeholder hop at `/go/home-water-test` for a future kit partner; a certified lab panel that includes iron, hardness, pH, and iron bacteria is still the safer sizing input.

If someone sizes a resin bed or an oxidizer off a napkin hardness guess, stop and get numbers first. SoftenerIndex’s hardness test-kit vs lab-test guide is a sibling topic when it lands on main — until then, prefer a state-certified lab panel over a sales demo alone.

## Iron is not one problem

The [Minnesota Department of Health (MDH) iron-in-well-water page](https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/environment/water/wells/waterquality/iron.html) is a clear public primer. SoftenerIndex is not a Minnesota site, but MDH’s form definitions are the same language dealers use everywhere:

- **Ferrous / “clear-water” iron** — water looks clear at the tap, then turns red or brown after standing.
- **Ferric / “red-water” iron** — water is already red or yellow when it leaves the faucet.
- **Organic iron** — often yellow or brown (sometimes colorless); more common in shallow wells or wells influenced by surface water.

MDH also notes iron usually is **not** a health risk at typical levels, can cause metallic taste and staining, and can clog equipment. As a **point of reference** (not SoftenerIndex’s invention), MDH says water above **0.3 mg/L** iron is usually considered objectionable, and that iron in water is usually below **10 mg/L**. Those figures belong to MDH’s page — verify them there, and treat your lab result as the number that sizes *your* system.

Ask the lab (or a second draw protocol your state recommends) how to sample so you are not guessing which form dominates. Oxidized iron that appears only after air contact is a different treatment conversation than particles already in the glass.

## Softener alone is not a universal iron fix

A conventional softener is a **cation-exchange** machine for hardness (calcium and magnesium). Some iron rides along; some does not.

MDH’s treatment section states, citing manufacturer reports rather than SoftenerIndex measurements:

- Softeners and iron filters (for example manganese greensand) can remove **clear-water** iron.
- Softeners are the more common method for that form; manufacturers report some units up to **10 mg/L**, with **2–5 mg/L** a more common softener limit.
- A manganese greensand-style iron filter is discussed for higher clear-water ranges (MDH cites up to **10–15 mg/L** in that context).
- Softeners remove hardness; iron can **plug resin** and needs periodic cleaning/backwash discipline. Softeners are **less effective** when hardness is low and iron is high, or when the system allows air contact (MDH calls out air-charged galvanized pressure tanks as an example).
- For **red-water** iron, MDH points to iron filters and, at higher levels, aeration or chemical oxidation followed by filtration — and warns that sediment filters, carbon, or softeners that see only “small amounts” of that iron can plug quickly.
- **Organic iron / tannins** can defeat softeners, aeration, and iron filters; oxidation-plus-filtration, distillation, or RO appear in MDH’s options list, with the caveat that high iron can plug point-of-use units fast.

Read those mg/L figures as **MDH summarizing the trade**, not as SoftenerIndex certifying your model. Demand the **model’s** published iron rating and the chemistry assumptions behind it.

## What “iron filter” usually means in a quote

Dealers use “iron filter” for several different machines:

1. **Oxidizing / catalytic media beds** (greensand and cousins) that need regeneration chemistry or a continuous oxidant story.
2. **Aeration / air-injection** systems that put oxygen into the water, then filter precipitated iron.
3. **Chemical feed** (chlorine or peroxide) plus contact time plus filtration — dosing mistakes have health and warranty consequences; MDH flags improper chemical oxidation dosing as risky.
4. **Combination softener-plus-iron** packages that share a tank or stack two vessels on one work order.

SoftenerIndex will not rank brands or invent which media “works to X ppm.” Ask which **mechanism** is on the bid, what **pre-treatment** (sediment, pH adjustment) it assumes, where **backwash / drain / air** goes, and what maintenance chemicals you must buy every year.

## Hardness and iron on the same well

EPA’s chart already pairs “softener for hardness” with a reminder to look at manganese and iron. Practically:

- Hardness without much iron may be a softener conversation ([salt-based vs salt-free](/guides/salt-based-vs-salt-free) still applies — do not accept a conditioner relabeled as a softener).
- Iron without much hardness may be a filter / oxidation conversation, not a softener upsell.
- Both present is where quotes get muddy: one box promised to “do everything,” or an iron stage skipped so the softener resin fouls in a season.

Order of treatment is a design question for a licensed water-conditioning contractor or plumber in your state — SoftenerIndex does not publish a universal piping diagram. What we do insist on is that the quote **names both problems** with lab numbers next to them.

## Certification families to look up (model, not brochure)

Existing SoftenerIndex guides point at **NSF/ANSI 44** for residential cation-exchange softeners. For aesthetic iron claims on filtration products, the industry certification family commonly referenced is **NSF/ANSI 42** (aesthetic effects; claim sets vary by product). Look up the **exact model** in a certification listing. “NSF certified line” without a model is not a claim you can audit.

EPA’s water-efficient softener guidance also steers buyers toward NSF/ANSI 44 listed softeners and demand-initiated regeneration — useful when the well job includes a softener stage, still not a price.

## What to demand on the work order

Walk the quote with [how to read a dealer quote](/guides/how-to-read-a-dealer-quote) and the [2026 fair-quote cost guide](/guides/water-softener-cost-2026). Add well-specific lines:

- Lab report date, lab name, and which analytes (iron form/total iron, hardness, pH, manganese, iron bacteria if tested).
- Softener brand/model, resin volume, grain capacity at a stated efficiency, and any **stated** iron tolerance from the manufacturer literature (attach or link it).
- Iron-treatment brand/model, media or oxidation method, backwash rate, drain plan, and chemical/air requirements.
- Whether iron treatment is **upstream** of the softener when both are sold — and what happens to the warranty if it is not.
- Electrical, well-pressure-tank interaction, and who is licensed for the work in your state.
- Exclusions: shock chlorination, well rehab, sediment pre-filters, pH correction, haul-away.

No SoftenerIndex page will invent an installed price for that stack. Empty price fields are intentional.

## What this page leaves empty on purpose

- No SoftenerIndex ppm cutoff table beyond citing MDH’s published ranges and telling you to verify the source.
- No installer roster (use your state’s license lookup the way city pages do).
- No brand decode, performance percentages, or “best under $X” list.
- No claim that your aquifer matches Minnesota’s — MDH is cited for forms and treatment *categories*, not for your geology.

Test, name the chemistry, then compare two written bids that solve the same lab result. That is the whole product.
