Dealers size softeners from a hardness number. SoftenerIndex city pages only publish that number when a named utility report is attached in frontmatter. The rest of the time you need *your* water measured — and the tool you pick (strip, titration kit, or certified lab) changes how much you should trust the digit you hand the salesperson.

This guide is about **which test answers which question**. It is not a product review, a kit ranking, or a price list. SoftenerIndex will not invent brand scores or dollar bands for test kits or lab panels.

## What you are actually measuring

Hardness is mostly dissolved calcium and magnesium, usually reported as grains per gallon (gpg) or milligrams per liter / parts per million as CaCO₃. A utility [Consumer Confidence Report](https://www.epa.gov/ccr) or water-quality summary can give a system-wide figure for public supply. That is context for the pipe network, not always the figure at your kitchen tap after softeners, filters, or private wells enter the picture.

SoftenerIndex leaves `hardnessGpg` / `hardnessMgL` blank on city pages (see [Phoenix](/az/phoenix)) until a primary source is attached. A blog that says “this town is 15 grains” without a named report is marketing, not a SoftenerIndex source.

## At-home kits: useful screens, not gospel

Strip and drop-titration kits are built for quick screening:

- **Strips** change color; you match a chart. Lighting, timing, and judgment move the result. Many kits report a band, not a precise gpg.
- **Drop titration** (soap or EDTA-style kits) can be tighter for hardness alone when you follow the instructions carefully, but they still assume clean technique and a kit that is not expired.
- **Multi-parameter strip packs** that claim metals, hardness, pH, and more in one dip are convenience products. Independent work such as [UMass Amherst research on home water test kits](https://www.umass.edu/engineering/news/kumpel-home-water-tests) has found wide variability across kits — especially for low-level metals — so treat surprising strip results as a reason to retest, not as a final sizing number.

Use a kit when you want a fast check before a softener conversation, a rough set-point to compare against a utility report, or a “is this house obviously hard?” screen. Do not use a color chart as the only input for a large equipment purchase if the quote hinges on one grain.

## Certified labs: when the number has to hold up

For private wells and for decisions that affect health or treatment design, public-health guidance points to laboratories — not the bathroom counter.

- The [CDC’s well-water testing guidelines](https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/safety/guidelines-for-testing-well-water.html) say to use a **state-certified laboratory**, and to test at least yearly for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH (plus local contaminants your health department flags).
- [EPA guidance for private wells](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) repeats the annual minimum panel and tells owners to use certified drinking-water labs. EPA does not test residential taps on request; states certify the labs.
- Find a lab through your state program via EPA’s [certified laboratory contacts](https://www.epa.gov/dwlabcert/contact-information-certification-programs-and-certified-laboratories-drinking-water) page (or your local health department).

Lab panels are the right tool when:

- You are on a **private well** (no utility CCR for your tap).
- A dealer’s pitch depends on a hardness / iron / manganese number you cannot verify.
- You need contaminants strips cannot resolve (bacteria, nitrate at infant-risk levels, lead, arsenic, PFAS, and similar).
- You want a written result with method and units you can keep next to the quote.

Ask the lab which analytes you ordered, what units they report hardness in, and how fresh the sample must be — especially for bacteria.

## Softener sizing: match the test to the job

Bring *something* measurable to the bid appointment. Prefer this order:

1. **Public supply:** current utility report or hardness note for your system or pressure zone, plus a kit check at the tap if the report is old or your neighborhood blends sources.
2. **Private well or inconclusive utility data:** state-certified lab hardness (and iron/manganese if staining or well chemistry is in play).
3. **Dealer “free test”:** treat it as their sales input. Ask for the method and the number in writing. Compare it to your own kit or lab result before you sign. Use [How to read a dealer quote](/guides/how-to-read-a-dealer-quote) and the [2026 fair-quote guide](/guides/water-softener-cost-2026).

Salt-based ion exchange removes hardness minerals; salt-free conditioners generally do not. Misreading a strip can push you into the wrong product class — see [salt-based vs salt-free](/guides/salt-based-vs-salt-free).

## Bottom line

Strips and titration kits are literacy tools: fast, cheap enough for most households to try, and easy to over-trust. State-certified labs are the audit trail when the number has to size equipment, protect a well household, or push back on a driveway pitch. SoftenerIndex would rather leave a field blank than decorate a page with an unsourced grain count.
